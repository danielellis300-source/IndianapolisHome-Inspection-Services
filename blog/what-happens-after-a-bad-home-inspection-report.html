What Happens After a Bad Home Inspection Report? Your Options in Indiana

April 29, 2026 · 3 min read

A long list of findings on an inspection report can feel alarming, especially for a first-time buyer seeing one for the first time — but most reports have more items than most buyers expect, and that's genuinely normal. Here's how to actually respond, and what your real options are once the report is in hand.

It helps to remember that every home, including the one you're currently living in, would generate a long list of findings if inspected today. The question was never whether a report would have items on it — it's whether any of those items are serious enough to change your decision.

First, separate the routine from the serious

Every inspection report lists dozens of small items — a loose handrail, a GFCI outlet that needs replacing, a bit of minor caulking. That's completely normal and not a reason to panic on its own. What actually matters are safety issues, structural concerns, and major system failures involving the roof, HVAC, electrical panel, or foundation. Read the report closely with your agent and, ideally, follow up with a call to the inspector directly to sort routine maintenance items from the handful that actually change your decision about the purchase.

Option 1: Request repairs

You can ask the seller to complete specific repairs before closing, typically documented in a formal repair addendum attached to the purchase agreement. This works best for well-defined, easily quotable items — a failed water heater, a broken window seal — and works considerably less well for vague or subjective concerns where "fixed" is hard to define clearly.

Option 2: Negotiate price or credit instead

Many Indianapolis buyers prefer a price reduction or a closing cost credit over requested repairs, since it puts you in full control of hiring your own contractor rather than trusting the seller to complete work to your standard on their own deadline. This is often the cleaner path for larger items like roof replacement or an HVAC system nearing the end of its life.

Option 3: Walk away

If your purchase agreement includes an inspection contingency, a serious enough finding gives you the contractual right to cancel and get your earnest money back in full. This is the right move for issues that are both expensive and fundamental to the home — significant structural movement, extensive mold, or a collapsed sewer line, for example — where no reasonable repair credit realistically makes the deal worth proceeding with anymore.

Option 4: Accept and move forward

Sometimes the right answer is simply proceeding as-is, especially in a competitive market where re-negotiating risks losing the home entirely, or when the findings are manageable and were already roughly priced into your initial offer. There's no universally correct answer here — it genuinely depends on the specific finding, the current market, and your own risk tolerance as a buyer. Key takeaways

  • Separate routine maintenance items from genuine safety, structural, and major system findings before deciding how to respond.
  • Repair requests work best for well-defined items; credits work better for larger or more subjective ones.
  • An inspection contingency gives you the contractual right to walk away and recover earnest money on serious findings.
  • There's no universally correct response — it depends on the finding, the market, and your own risk tolerance.
