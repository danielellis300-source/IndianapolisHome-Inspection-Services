Pre-Purchase Inspections for Indianapolis Real Estate Investors: What to Prioritize

July 23, 2026 · 3 min read

An investor's inspection priorities aren't quite the same as an owner-occupant's. You're generally less concerned with cosmetic condition and far more concerned with the systems that drive holding costs and renovation budgets over the entire life of the investment. Here's how to approach it with that lens specifically.

The stakes are also different than for an owner-occupant: a missed defect on a personal home is a personal headache, but a missed defect on an investment property directly erodes your projected return and can turn a promising deal into a break-even one once real repair costs come in.

Treating the inspection as a core part of your underwriting process, not a formality to clear before closing, is what separates investors who hit their numbers from those who don't.

This is especially true when competing against cash buyers who may be tempted to skip inspections entirely — a fast, well-organized inspection process lets you move quickly without cutting that corner.

Build the inspection into your acquisition timeline, not around it

On a tight closing timeline — common with off-market or auction-sourced deals — it's tempting to shorten or skip the inspection entirely just to keep pace with the deal. Instead, build inspection scheduling into your offer terms from the very start of the process. A same-day or next-day inspection turnaround, which we offer regularly for exactly this reason, keeps your timeline intact without actually skipping real due diligence.

Prioritize the big-ticket systems

Roof age and remaining life, HVAC age and condition, electrical panel capacity and overall condition, and — critically for older Marion County housing stock — sewer line material and condition should all get explicit, focused attention. These four categories are the ones most likely to turn a carefully projected renovation budget into a blown one after the fact.

Get a sewer scope on anything pre-1980

This is worth calling out specifically for investors: a $150–$225 sewer scope on an older acquisition is one of the highest-ROI due diligence dollars you can possibly spend on the deal. A collapsed or root-choked lateral discovered after closing, versus before you're committed, is the difference between a clean negotiated price adjustment and an unplanned $10,000+ hit straight to your rehab budget.

Use the report to build your real repair budget

A thorough inspection report is a far better starting point for building a renovation budget than a quick walkthrough estimate ever will be. Line up findings against your contractor bids category by category — roof, HVAC, electrical, plumbing — to catch any gaps between what the report actually flagged and what your contractor ultimately priced out.

Know your deal-breakers before you're under contract

Decide in advance what would genuinely kill a deal for you — significant foundation movement, a sewer line requiring full replacement, knob-and-tube wiring running throughout the whole house — so you're not making that call emotionally in the middle of a tight closing timeline with other pressures competing for your attention. Key takeaways

  • Build inspection scheduling into your offer terms rather than shortening due diligence to save time.
  • Roof, HVAC, electrical panel, and sewer line condition drive the majority of unplanned renovation costs.
  • A $150–$225 sewer scope is one of the highest-ROI due diligence dollars on any pre-1980 acquisition.
  • Decide your deal-breakers before you're under contract so you're not deciding emotionally under time pressure.
