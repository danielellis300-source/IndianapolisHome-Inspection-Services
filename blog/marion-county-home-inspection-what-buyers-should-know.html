Marion County Home Buying: What Local Home Age and Housing Stock Mean for Your Inspection

July 23, 2026 · 3 min read

Marion County's housing stock spans well over a century, from early-1900s bungalows in Irvington to brand-new infill construction downtown. The decade a home was built tells an inspector — and should tell you — a lot about what to expect before you ever walk through the door for a showing.

This isn't just trivia — knowing what era-specific issues to expect changes how you shop. A buyer eyeing a 1968 ranch in Broad Ripple should be asking different questions than a buyer touring a 2022 build in Westfield, even if both homes look equally well maintained on a showing.

Pre-1960s homes: original systems are the main risk

Homes in older Indianapolis neighborhoods like Irvington, Fountain Square, and Meridian-Kessler often still carry some original infrastructure: clay tile sewer laterals, galvanized steel supply plumbing (which corrodes from the inside and gradually restricts water flow over decades), knob-and-tube wiring remnants in older sections, and undersized electrical panels never upgraded past 60 or 100 amps. None of these automatically fail an inspection, but all of them warrant a closer look and, often, a budget line for near-term upgrades once you own the home.

1960s–1970s homes: aluminum wiring

Homes built roughly between 1965 and 1973 sometimes used aluminum branch circuit wiring instead of copper, a cost-saving measure at the time that's since been linked to a higher risk of loose connections and overheating at outlets and switches as connections age. It's not an automatic dealbreaker, but it's worth having evaluated by a licensed electrician before closing, and it can affect insurability with some carriers depending on whether proper remediation (like copper pigtailing) has already been done.

1980s–1990s homes: polybutylene plumbing

Gray polybutylene supply piping was widely used in this era before it was pulled from the market due to a tendency to fail from the inside out, often without warning and sometimes years after installation. If your target home falls in this build range, ask specifically whether the supply lines have been inspected or replaced — this is a system-level issue an inspector can flag by fixture type and visible piping, but full verification sometimes requires opening a wall or accessing a crawl space directly.

What's consistent across every era: soil and grading

Regardless of build decade, Marion County's clay-heavy soil drains slowly and expands and contracts with moisture, which is the leading cause of foundation cracking, basement seepage, and drainage issues we see across the county, from century-old bungalows to homes built last year. Grading that slopes toward the house instead of away from it is one of the most common — and thankfully cheapest to fix — issues found during inspections here, regardless of the home's age.

The practical takeaway

Knowing the decade a home was built lets you ask sharper questions before you even schedule the inspection. Bring up the home's approximate build year when you book with us, and we'll make sure the visit pays extra attention to whatever's typical for that era. For a broader look at what turns up most often locally across all housing stock, see our roundup of the most common issues found in Indianapolis inspections.

Key takeaways

  • Pre-1960s homes often still have galvanized plumbing, clay tile sewer laterals, and undersized electrical panels.
  • Homes built 1965–1973 may have aluminum branch wiring, which warrants evaluation by a licensed electrician.
  • 1980s–1990s homes sometimes have polybutylene supply piping, which is prone to failing from the inside out.
  • Regardless of build decade, Marion County's clay soil is the leading cause of foundation and drainage issues countywide.
