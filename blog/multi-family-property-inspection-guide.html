Multi-Family Property Inspections: What Indianapolis Investors Need to Know

July 23, 2026 · 3 min read

Duplexes, triplexes, and small multi-family buildings inspect quite differently than a single-family home — mainly because of shared systems and the need to evaluate multiple units consistently, unit by unit, rather than treating the whole building as one property. Here's what actually changes in scope and approach.

Getting the inspection scope right up front also matters for financing — many lenders on multi-family deals want to see a professional inspection report covering both individual units and shared systems before they'll finalize loan terms, so building extra time in for this isn't just good practice, it's often a closing requirement.

Working with an inspector who's comfortable coordinating access across multiple tenants and explaining shared-system findings clearly makes the whole process considerably smoother for everyone involved.

Pricing scales with units, not just square footage

Expect a duplex to cost roughly 1.5–1.8x a comparable single-family inspection, and a triplex or fourplex to run closer to 2–2.5x, since each unit's kitchen, bathrooms, and mechanical systems need to be evaluated individually even where total square footage overlaps meaningfully with a single-family comparison.

Shared vs. separate systems matter a lot

Some multi-family properties have a single shared furnace or water heater serving multiple units at once; others have fully separate mechanical systems for each individual unit. This distinction directly affects both your ongoing operating costs as an owner and what needs focused, individual attention during the inspection itself — a shared system failure affects every tenant at once, which raises the stakes considerably on catching issues early rather than after a complaint.

Consistency across units reveals patterns

Inspecting all units in the same visit, with the same inspector doing the walkthrough, makes it much easier to spot a building-wide issue — like consistent moisture intrusion on one side of the structure, or aging original wiring running throughout — versus a problem that's genuinely unit-specific. That distinction matters enormously for budgeting purposes, since a building-wide issue is really a capital expense while a unit-specific one is just a normal line-item repair.

Common areas need their own scope

Shared hallways, stairwells, laundry areas, and the roof — which is almost always shared even when the units themselves are otherwise fully separate — should be explicitly included in the inspection scope from the start. These shared areas carry liability exposure that single-family owners simply don't have to think about; a stairwell handrail or lighting issue is a habitability and safety concern across every unit in the building at once, not just one tenant's space.

Occupied units require coordination

If some or all units are currently tenant-occupied, plan ahead for advance notice and scheduling coordination — Indiana's reasonable notice expectations for landlord entry apply here just as they would for a routine inspection of an occupied single-family rental. Building this coordination time into your due diligence timeline up front avoids a rushed, incomplete inspection later when access turns out to be harder to arrange than expected. Key takeaways

  • Duplexes typically cost 1.5–1.8x a comparable single-family inspection; triplex/fourplex runs closer to 2–2.5x.
  • Shared mechanical systems raise the stakes, since one failure can affect every tenant in the building at once.
  • Inspecting all units in one visit makes it easier to spot building-wide issues versus unit-specific ones.
  • Common areas like stairwells, hallways, and the roof carry liability exposure and need their own explicit scope.
