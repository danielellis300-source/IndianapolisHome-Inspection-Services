First-Time Home Buyer's Guide to Inspections in Indianapolis

May 11, 2026 · 3 min read

If this is your first time buying a home, the inspection can feel like the most opaque part of the entire process — a stranger walks through your future home for a few hours and hands you a report full of terms you've never heard before. Here's a plain-language walkthrough of what to actually expect, from booking to closing day.

It's normal to feel a little overwhelmed the first time through this process — every buyer does. Knowing roughly what to expect at each stage, from booking to reading the final report, takes a lot of the anxiety out of what can otherwise feel like a black box.

When to schedule it

Schedule your inspection as soon as your offer is accepted, ideally that same day — most Indiana purchase agreements give you a limited inspection contingency window, often 7–10 days, and that clock starts running immediately once the contract is signed. Booking right away, rather than waiting a few days to "settle in" to the excitement, protects your ability to actually use that full window if scheduling gets tight for any reason.

What to bring and what to expect

Wear clothes you don't mind getting a little dusty if you plan to follow the inspector into the attic access area or down into the basement. Expect the visit itself to run 2–4 hours for a typical single-family home, longer for larger properties. Bring a notepad or just your phone — you'll want to jot down questions as they come up in the moment rather than trying to remember everything for later once you're home.

Should you attend?

Yes, if at all possible, and we mean that. Reading a written report afterward is useful, but seeing an issue in person — with the inspector explaining what it actually means and how serious it really is in context — gives you a level of understanding that's genuinely hard to get from a PDF document alone. It's also your best real opportunity to ask "is this actually a big deal or not" in the moment, rather than guessing later.

Understanding the report

Reports typically organize findings by system (roof, electrical, plumbing, HVAC, structure) with a severity indication and supporting photos for each item. Don't panic at the total item count you see on the summary page — a 40-item report on an older home is completely normal and expected. Focus your attention specifically on anything flagged as a safety concern, a major system failure, or something structural in nature rather than every single line.

After the report

Talk through the findings with your real estate agent to decide whether to request repairs, negotiate a credit, or proceed as-is — see our guide on what happens after a bad inspection report for how that decision typically plays out for buyers in your position. Whatever you ultimately decide, keep the full report on file; it's a genuinely useful record of the home's exact condition at purchase if anything comes up unexpectedly down the road.

Key takeaways

  • Book your inspection the same day your offer is accepted — your contingency window starts immediately.
  • Attend if at all possible; seeing findings in person beats reading about them later in a PDF.
  • A long item count on the report is normal — focus on safety, structural, and major system findings.
  • Keep the final report on file as a documented record of the home's condition at purchase.
