Can You Negotiate Repairs After a Home Inspection in Indiana?

May 5, 2026 · 3 min read

Yes — but "can you" and "should you" are genuinely different questions, and how you ask matters almost as much as what you ask for. Here's how repair negotiations typically play out in practice across the Indianapolis market.

The tone of the request matters almost as much as the substance. A repair request framed as a reasonable, well-documented ask tends to get a better response from a seller than one that reads like a list of demands, even when the underlying items are identical.

Working closely with your agent on how to frame requests, and being realistic about what's likely to succeed given current market conditions, tends to produce better outcomes than an aggressive opening ask.

It depends on your contract's inspection contingency

Most Indiana purchase agreements include an inspection contingency period, giving buyers a defined window to complete inspections and then request repairs, a credit, or a price reduction — or to cancel the contract entirely. The specific terms of that window are negotiated as part of your original offer, so read this section of your contract closely well before you're actually relying on it during a live negotiation.

What sellers typically agree to

Safety issues (exposed wiring, missing handrails, a suspected gas leak) and major system failures (a non-functioning furnace, a failed water heater) are the items sellers most commonly agree to address, because they're objective, well-documented in the report, and reasonably straightforward to price and fix. Cosmetic items and normal wear and tear are far less likely to get much traction in a negotiation.

Repairs vs. credits: which to ask for

Asking for a closing credit instead of a completed repair is often the stronger move, especially for anything beyond a genuinely simple fix. It avoids later disputes over repair quality or whether the work was actually done correctly, lets you choose your own trusted contractor, and keeps the whole timeline in your control instead of dependent on the seller scheduling and completing work before your closing date.

Market conditions change the leverage

In a competitive seller's market, aggressive repair requests on minor items can put the entire deal at risk — a seller with other interested buyers waiting in the wings has very little incentive to negotiate hard over small stuff. In a slower market, or on a home that's sat on the market for a while already, buyers generally have considerably more room to negotiate meaningfully.

Document everything

Whatever you ultimately negotiate, get it in writing as a formal addendum to the purchase agreement — verbal agreements between agents don't hold up well if something falls through before closing day arrives. If repairs are agreed to rather than a credit, request receipts or a completion walkthrough before closing whenever it's realistically possible to schedule one. For the bigger picture on how to respond to a rough report overall, see our guide on what happens after a bad inspection report.

Key takeaways

  • Your ability to negotiate depends entirely on the inspection contingency terms in your specific purchase agreement.
  • Sellers are most likely to agree to safety issues and major system failures, less likely on cosmetic items.
  • A closing credit is often a stronger ask than a completed repair, since it keeps the contractor choice in your hands.
  • Get any agreed repairs or credits in writing as a formal addendum — verbal agreements don't hold up at closing.
